Scope, notation & how to read the derivation

Evidence → postulates → deductions → computation → limits. History explains why the assumptions were proposed; it does not prove them. Topics 05–11 solve the nonrelativistic two-body Coulomb problem. The site then takes the fixed-proton limit and displays only bound shells \(n\le6\). Later topics identify the extra physics needed outside that model.

Ordinary calculus, complex numbers, vectors and basic linear algebra are assumed. A hat denotes an operator; \(|\psi\rangle\) is a state and \(\langle\phi|\psi\rangle\) its inner product. The physical constants are \(\hbar=h/(2\pi)\), positive elementary charge \(e\), electron mass \(m_e\), proton mass \(m_p\), vacuum permittivity \(\varepsilon_0\), and speed of light \(c\). We write \(\kappa=e^2/(4\pi\varepsilon_0)\), reduced mass \(\mu=m_em_p/(m_e+m_p)\), and \(a_\mu=\hbar^2/(\mu\kappa)\). The fixed-proton Bohr radius is \(a_0=\hbar^2/(m_e\kappa)\).

Symbols reused locally are defined where needed: \(\rho\) is a dimensionless radial coordinate in the bound-state derivation and a probability density in the current discussion; \(q\) denotes a magnetic magnitude in the harmonic construction and a dipole component in the transition calculation. Frequencies \(\nu\) and \(\omega=2\pi\nu\) differ by \(2\pi\). Energies are relative to the separated-particle threshold unless stated otherwise.

Long equations and tables scroll horizontally on narrow screens. Source links accompany each deep dive. Historical formulas are presented in modern notation; dated research examples in the final topics distinguish established effects from active investigations.

01

From atoms as an idea to the nuclear atom

Chemistry, spectral lines and scattering supplied different pieces of the problem. A compact positive nucleus explains scattering; classical motion around it does not explain a stable atom. These observations motivate the quantum postulates used later rather than logically proving them.

\[V(r)=-\frac{\kappa}{r},\qquad \kappa=\frac{e^2}{4\pi\varepsilon_0};\qquad P_{\rm rad}=\frac{e^2a^2}{6\pi\varepsilon_0c^3}.\]
History & derivationAtomism, chemical evidence, electrons and the classical stability problem

A sequence of questions, not a sequence of literal pictures

Ancient atomism, associated in the Greek tradition with Leucippus and Democritus, proposed that matter could be composed of indivisible constituents moving in empty space. It was a philosophical proposal, not a calculation of hydrogen or an experimentally established account of electrons. In the early nineteenth century, John Dalton connected atoms to chemical combining ratios. His New System of Chemical Philosophy, beginning in 1808, made atomic weights and small whole-number combinations central to chemical reasoning. Those ideas organized chemistry without specifying an atom's internal electrical structure; some of Dalton's molecular assignments were incorrect.

Hydrogen supplied another constraint: heated or excited gas emits distinct spectral lines rather than every possible color. Balmer's 1885 empirical pattern for visible hydrogen lines expressed wavelengths through integer squares. The later Rydberg form writes the inverse wavelength as a difference of two terms. This was a description of measured regularities before a successful dynamical explanation existed. A model had to explain why these integers appeared.

Why the nucleus mattered

J. J. Thomson's 1897 cathode-ray work established negatively charged constituents much lighter than atoms. His later distributed-positive-charge models attempted to combine those electrons into neutral structures. Geiger and Marsden's large-angle alpha-particle scattering, interpreted by Rutherford in 1911, instead indicated a concentrated source of strong electric fields. The nuclear picture supplied the center of attraction needed for the Coulomb potential above.

For a heavy point nucleus of charge \(Ze\), the classical repulsive scattering of an alpha particle with charge \(ze\), kinetic energy \(K\) and impact parameter \(b\) gives

\[b=\frac{zZe^2}{8\pi\varepsilon_0K}\cot\frac{\theta}{2},\qquad \frac{\mathrm d\sigma}{\mathrm d\Omega}=\frac{b}{\sin\theta}\left|\frac{\mathrm db}{\mathrm d\theta}\right|=\left(\frac{zZe^2}{16\pi\varepsilon_0K}\right)^2\frac1{\sin^4(\theta/2)}.\]

The cross section follows by equating an annulus of incident trajectories, \(2\pi b\,\mathrm db\), to the scattered solid-angle ring \(2\pi\sin\theta\,\mathrm d\theta\). This ideal result assumes a heavy target, Coulomb scattering and negligible screening over the relevant close encounter. It explains why rare large deflections reveal concentrated charge. It does not determine electron orbitals.

Where a classical hydrogen atom fails

For a circular electron orbit about a fixed proton, Newton's law gives \(m_ev^2/r=\kappa/r^2\). Hence \(T=\kappa/(2r)\) and \(E=T+V=-\kappa/(2r)\). The accelerating charge radiates according to the nonrelativistic Larmor formula. With \(a=\kappa/(m_er^2)\) and \(\mathrm dE/\mathrm dt=-P_{\rm rad}\),

\[\frac{\kappa}{2r^2}\frac{\mathrm dr}{\mathrm dt}=-\frac{e^2\kappa^2}{6\pi\varepsilon_0m_e^2c^3r^4},\qquad \frac{\mathrm dr}{\mathrm dt}=-\frac{e^2\kappa}{3\pi\varepsilon_0m_e^2c^3r^2}.\]

The radius decreases instead of remaining stable. Integrating the circular-orbit estimate predicts collapse from an atomic-scale radius in roughly \(10^{-11}\) seconds. The approximation fails near the center, but its conclusion is decisive: classical electrodynamics supplies no stable, nonradiating planetary ground state. Nor does a continuum of classical radii explain discrete line energies.

02

Energy quanta and the photoelectric effect

Planck introduced an energy scale proportional to frequency. Einstein extended the quantum idea to light itself. Conservation of energy then links a photon's frequency to electron emission and, later, to atomic transitions.

\[E_\gamma=h\nu=\hbar\omega=\frac{hc}{\lambda},\qquad K_{\max}=h\nu-\Phi,\qquad \nu_0=\frac{\Phi}{h}.\]
History & derivationPlanck's spectrum, Einstein's light quanta and threshold energy

Planck's 1900 step

Blackbody radiation exposed a second failure of classical reasoning. A cavity has electromagnetic modes with frequency density \(8\pi\nu^2/c^3\) per volume. Giving each mode the classical mean energy \(k_BT\) predicts \(u_\nu=8\pi\nu^2k_BT/c^3\), whose integral diverges at high frequency. Planck's 1900 energy-element prescription instead used packets \(h\nu\). His original argument used resonator entropy and combinatorial counting; the following compact derivation is a modern statistical reconstruction, not a transcription of his reasoning.

Assume an oscillator's excitation energies are \(E_j=jh\nu\), with \(j=0,1,\ldots\), and canonical probabilities proportional to \(\exp(-\beta E_j)\), where \(\beta=1/(k_BT)\). Sum the geometric series and differentiate:

\[Z=\sum_{j=0}^{\infty}\mathrm e^{-\beta jh\nu}=\frac1{1-\mathrm e^{-\beta h\nu}},\qquad \langle E\rangle=-\frac{\partial\ln Z}{\partial\beta}=\frac{h\nu}{\mathrm e^{\beta h\nu}-1}.\]
\[u_\nu(T)=\frac{8\pi h\nu^3}{c^3}\frac1{\mathrm e^{h\nu/(k_BT)}-1}.\]

When \(h\nu\ll k_BT\), expand the exponential to recover the classical result. At high frequency the exponential suppresses the population, making the total thermal energy finite. The zero-point energy of the modern oscillator is not included in this thermal radiation density.

Einstein's 1905 step

Einstein proposed localized light energy quanta and applied them to photoelectric emission. In the simplest single-photon surface model, one electron absorbs \(h\nu\), spends at least the work function \(\Phi\) escaping the material, and leaves with at most \(K_{\max}=h\nu-\Phi\). The stopping-potential magnitude satisfies \(eV_s=K_{\max}\). Thus the slope of \(V_s\) against frequency is \(h/e\), while the threshold depends on the material.

In this regime, increasing intensity at fixed frequency increases photon arrival rate and usually the emitted-electron count; it does not raise the maximum energy of each electron. No single-photon emission occurs below threshold. Multiphoton and strong-field processes lie outside that simple statement.

What carries over to hydrogen

A metal work function is not a hydrogen energy level. For an isolated hydrogen atom, the corresponding one-photon ionization balance is \(h\nu=|E_n|+K\), apart from recoil and corrections. For a bound-to-bound transition it is \(h\nu=|E_u-E_l|\). These relations connect a measured wavelength to an energy difference. They do not by themselves determine which transition is allowed or how strongly it couples; that requires wavefunctions and an interaction matrix element.

Primary accounts: Planck, 1900 · radiation-law derivation; Planck's retrospective Nobel lecture; Library of Congress · Einstein's 1905 papers and scholarly translations.

03

Bohr's atom and the spectral energy ladder

Bohr's 1913 model combined classical Coulomb mechanics with nonclassical stationary states and a photon-frequency rule. It recovered the hydrogen energy ladder before a wavefunction theory existed.

\[r_n=a_\mu n^2,\qquad E_n=-\frac{\mu \kappa^2}{2\hbar^2n^2},\qquad \frac1\lambda=R_\mu\left(\frac1{n_l^2}-\frac1{n_u^2}\right).\]
History & derivationQuantized circular orbits, reduced mass and the Rydberg formula

State the extra assumptions

For an electron and proton, let \(\mu=m_em_p/(m_e+m_p)\) be their reduced mass and let \(r\) be their separation. In the circular-orbit version, impose \(L=\mu vr=n\hbar\), assume these selected orbits do not radiate, and require emitted or absorbed light to obey \(h\nu=|E_u-E_l|\). The nonradiation rule explicitly suspends the classical conclusion of section 01. These quantum conditions are inputs, not consequences of Newton's laws.

Derive the radius and energy

Coulomb attraction provides the centripetal force. Eliminate the speed between the force balance and angular-momentum condition:

\[\frac{\mu v^2}{r}=\frac{\kappa}{r^2},\qquad v=\frac{n\hbar}{\mu r} \quad\Longrightarrow\quad r_n=\frac{n^2\hbar^2}{\mu \kappa}=a_\mu n^2,\qquad a_\mu=\frac{\hbar^2}{\mu \kappa}.\]

The force balance also gives \(T=\kappa/(2r)\). Therefore \(E=T-\kappa/r=-\kappa/(2r)\), and insertion of \(r_n\) produces the negative inverse-square energy ladder. The zero of energy is the separated electron and proton at rest. As \(n\to\infty\), the binding tends to zero and the levels approach the ionization threshold.

Recover the spectral pattern

\[\frac{hc}{\lambda}=E_{n_u}-E_{n_l}=\frac{\mu \kappa^2}{2\hbar^2}\left(\frac1{n_l^2}-\frac1{n_u^2}\right),\qquad R_\mu=\frac{\mu \kappa^2}{2\hbar^2hc}.\]

Fixing \(n_l=2\) gives the Balmer family. Algebraically, \(\lambda=(4/R_\mu)n_u^2/(n_u^2-4)\), the integer-square pattern that Balmer had inferred from measurements. Other fixed lower levels give Lyman, Paschen, Brackett and further families. These equations use vacuum wavelength; historical measured wavelengths and modern precision tables require attention to air corrections and finer atomic effects.

With an infinitely massive proton, \(\mu\to m_e\), \(a_\mu\to a_0\), and the binding-energy coefficient is approximately \(13.606\,\mathrm{eV}\). Finite proton mass changes the coefficient by \(\mu/m_e\). The site's base Coulomb model uses the electron-mass scale, so it should not be treated as a precision line database.

Keep the success, replace the orbit

Bohr recovered the leading energies but not the modern orbital shapes, transition matrix elements, spin or a general theory of multielectron atoms. Its \(L=n\hbar\) is not the exact hydrogen angular-momentum spectrum: modern states have \(L^2=\hbar^2\ell(\ell+1)\), including a ground state with \(\ell=0\). Sommerfeld's later quantized elliptical orbits extended the old quantum theory, but its rules were superseded by quantum mechanics. The atlas visualizes wavefunction density, not Bohr trajectories.

04

Matter waves and the route to Schrödinger

De Broglie's matter-wave relation turns quantization into a wave-compatibility question. Combining wave generators with nonrelativistic energy suggests Schrödinger's equation; accepting that equation and its probability interpretation is the next physical postulate.

\[\lambda=\frac hp,\qquad \mathbf p=\hbar\mathbf k,\qquad E=\hbar\omega,\qquad \mathrm i\hbar\partial_t\Psi=\left[-\frac{\hbar^2}{2\mu}\nabla^2+V\right]\Psi.\]
History & derivationStanding waves, dispersion and the limits of a heuristic deduction

De Broglie's bridge

De Broglie proposed matter waves in 1923 and developed the proposal in his 1924 thesis. Extending the photon relations \(E=h\nu\) and \(p=h/\lambda\) to material particles linked momentum to spatial wavelength. In an illustrative circular standing-wave construction, the phase must return to its original value after one turn:

\[2\pi r=n\lambda\quad\Longrightarrow\quad pr=\frac{nh}{2\pi}=n\hbar.\]

This reproduces Bohr's circular angular-momentum condition. It is a useful historical bridge, not the exact three-dimensional hydrogen solution: a wave on a ring has a different domain and different boundary conditions from a Coulomb wave in space.

From plane waves to a candidate evolution law

For a free-particle wave \(\Psi=A\exp[\mathrm i(\mathbf k\cdot\mathbf r-\omega t)]\), differentiation yields \(\mathrm i\hbar\partial_t\Psi=\hbar\omega\Psi\) and \(-\mathrm i\hbar\nabla\Psi=\hbar\mathbf k\Psi\). Apply the nonrelativistic dispersion relation \(E=p^2/(2\mu)\):

\[\hbar\omega=\frac{\hbar^2k^2}{2\mu}\quad\Longrightarrow\quad \mathrm i\hbar\partial_t\Psi=-\frac{\hbar^2}{2\mu}\nabla^2\Psi.\]

Linearity extends this equation to superpositions of free plane waves. Adding a local potential-energy operator gives the displayed Schrödinger equation. This construction motivates its form; it does not deduce quantum mechanics from classical mechanics. The physical interpretation of the complex amplitude, the choice of operators, and the validity of linear unitary evolution require independent postulates and experimental tests.

What changed in 1925–1926

Heisenberg's matrix mechanics in 1925 organized observable transition quantities without classical electron trajectories. Schrödinger's 1926 wave mechanics expressed quantization as an eigenvalue problem and connected it to de Broglie's waves; the two formalisms proved equivalent. Born's probability interpretation in 1926 supplied the statistical meaning of the wavefunction. In the position representation used here, \(|\Psi|^2\,\mathrm d^3r\) is the probability of a position outcome in a small volume, not a photograph of a material electron spread through the atom.

For a free wave packet, \(v_g=\mathrm d\omega/\mathrm dk=\hbar k/\mu=p/\mu\), matching classical speed in the nonrelativistic limit. For a bound atom, constructive wave compatibility is imposed at the origin, at infinity and around the angular coordinates. The next sections derive the resulting states. Historical motivations come first here; the deductions that follow begin from explicit quantum assumptions.

05

The quantum starting point

A wavefunction, a probability rule and a Hamiltonian replace a classical electron trajectory. These are empirically tested postulates; the following deductions are conditional on them.

\[\int |\Psi|^2\,\mathrm d^3r=1,\qquad \langle A\rangle=\langle\Psi|\hat A|\Psi\rangle\]
\[\mathrm i\hbar\partial_t\Psi=\hat H\Psi,\qquad \hat{\mathbf p}=-\mathrm i\hbar\nabla\]
Derivation & detailsWhat is assumed, what follows, and why the equation is linear

State and measurement

For one spinless particle, a pure state is a normalized complex function in the Hilbert space of square-integrable functions. Multiplying it by a constant phase changes no measurement probabilities. The Born rule assigns probability \(|\Psi(\mathbf r,t)|^2\mathrm d^3r\) to a small volume. An observable is represented by a self-adjoint operator; its eigenvalues are possible sharp outcomes. A general state need not have a definite energy, position or angular momentum.

Continuous spatial translations identify momentum as their generator. In the position representation, translating a wavefunction by a small vector gives \(\Psi(\mathbf r-\mathbf a)\simeq\Psi-\mathbf a\cdot\nabla\Psi\). Comparing this with \(\exp(-\mathrm i\mathbf a\cdot\hat{\mathbf p}/\hbar)\) gives the momentum operator above and \([\hat x_i,\hat p_j]=\mathrm i\hbar\delta_{ij}\).

Time evolution

Preserving inner products and allowing superposition leads to unitary time evolution. For a time-independent Hamiltonian, \(U(t)=\exp(-\mathrm i\hat Ht/\hbar)\). Differentiating \(\Psi(t)=U(t)\Psi(0)\) gives the Schrödinger equation. This is a deduction from the quantum evolution postulate, not a proof of quantum mechanics from Newton's laws.

\[\hat H=\frac{\hat{\mathbf p}^{\,2}}{2m}+V(\mathbf r)\quad\Longrightarrow\quad \mathrm i\hbar\partial_t\Psi=\left[-\frac{\hbar^2}{2m}\nabla^2+V\right]\Psi.\]

For a free plane wave \(\exp[\mathrm i(\mathbf k\cdot\mathbf r-\omega t)]\), this equation returns \(E=\hbar\omega\), \(\mathbf p=\hbar\mathbf k\), and \(E=p^2/(2m)\). This consistency with de Broglie's relation motivates the equation, but the plane-wave argument alone does not establish the Born rule, operator interpretation or full interacting theory.

Why a sharply defined path is not part of the state

The position–momentum commutator has a measurable consequence. For centered operators \(\delta A=\hat A-\langle A\rangle\) and \(\delta B=\hat B-\langle B\rangle\), Cauchy–Schwarz applied to \(\delta A|\Psi\rangle\) and \(\delta B|\Psi\rangle\) gives \(\Delta A\Delta B\ge|\langle[\hat A,\hat B]\rangle|/2\). Thus \(\Delta x\Delta p_x\ge\hbar/2\). This is a property of quantum-state spreads, not merely poor instruments.

A useful scale estimate confines an electron to a region of size \(a\), giving momentum of order \(\hbar/a\). Its energy is then of order \(\hbar^2/(2\mu a^2)-\kappa/a\). Minimizing yields \(a\sim a_\mu\). This explains why localization costs enough kinetic energy to resist collapse. It is a dimensional estimate, not the exact ground-state derivation; the radial boundary-value problem below supplies that result.

Stationary does not mean motionless

For a time-independent potential, put \(\Psi=\psi T\). Division separates a purely temporal expression from a spatial one, so each equals a constant \(E\):

\[\hat H\psi=E\psi,\qquad T(t)=\mathrm e^{-\mathrm iEt/\hbar}.\]

The global time factor disappears from \(|\Psi|^2\). Thus an energy eigenstate has stationary probability density, although it may carry probability current. The visible cloud is a probability distribution, not a photograph of a material electron smeared through space.

06

Two particles become one relative coordinate

Hydrogen is an electron and a proton. Separating center-of-mass motion leaves a Coulomb problem with reduced mass; the renderer uses the simpler fixed-proton limit.

\[\hat H_{\mathrm{rel}}=-\frac{\hbar^2}{2\mu}\nabla_r^2-\frac{\kappa}{r},\qquad \mu=\frac{m_em_p}{m_e+m_p},\quad\kappa=\frac{e^2}{4\pi\varepsilon_0}\]
\[a_\mu=\frac{\hbar^2}{\mu\kappa},\qquad a_0=\frac{\hbar^2}{m_e\kappa}\]
Derivation & detailsCoulomb interaction, coordinate transformation and model assumptions

Specify the physical approximation

Assume an isolated electron and proton with nonrelativistic kinetic energies and instantaneous electrostatic interaction. Ignore spin, external fields, radiation and finite proton size. Opposite charges make the potential attractive: \(V=-\kappa/r\), with zero energy assigned to infinite separation. These approximations define the model being solved.

\[\hat H=-\frac{\hbar^2}{2m_e}\nabla_e^2-\frac{\hbar^2}{2m_p}\nabla_p^2-\frac{\kappa}{|\mathbf r_e-\mathbf r_p|}.\]

Change coordinates explicitly

Let \(M=m_e+m_p\), \(\mathbf R=(m_e\mathbf r_e+m_p\mathbf r_p)/M\) and \(\mathbf r=\mathbf r_e-\mathbf r_p\). The chain rule gives

\[\nabla_e=\frac{m_e}{M}\nabla_R+\nabla_r,\qquad \nabla_p=\frac{m_p}{M}\nabla_R-\nabla_r.\]

Squaring and adding with the inverse masses cancels the mixed derivatives. The coefficient of \(\nabla_R^2\) becomes \(1/M\); the coefficient of \(\nabla_r^2\) is \(1/m_e+1/m_p=1/\mu\). Hence

\[\hat H=-\frac{\hbar^2}{2M}\nabla_R^2+\hat H_{\mathrm{rel}},\qquad \Psi(\mathbf R,\mathbf r)=\mathrm e^{\mathrm i\mathbf K\cdot\mathbf R}\psi(\mathbf r).\]

The total energy is \(\hbar^2K^2/(2M)+E_{\mathrm{rel}}\). Atomic structure concerns the second term. This separation is exact within the stated two-body Coulomb model; holding the proton fixed is a further approximation.

The natural scales

Balancing a kinetic scale \(\hbar^2/(\mu a^2)\) against a Coulomb scale \(\kappa/a\) identifies \(a=a_\mu\). In dimensionless coordinates \(\mathbf x=\mathbf r/a_\mu\), divide the Hamiltonian by \(E_{\mathrm h,\mu}=\mu\kappa^2/\hbar^2\) to obtain \(-\nabla_x^2/2-1/x\). All dimensional constants have become length and energy units.

The site takes \(\mu=m_e\), measures distance in \(a_0\), and uses \(13.605693\,\mathrm{eV}\) as its binding-energy scale. Real finite-mass hydrogen has \(a_\mu=(m_e/\mu)a_0\) and energies smaller in magnitude by \(\mu/m_e\). Replacing the proton mass with a different nuclear mass explains the leading isotope shift without changing the mathematical method.

07

Rotational symmetry determines angular shape

A central potential permits simultaneous energy and angular-momentum eigenstates. Single-valuedness and regularity determine the spherical harmonics and the allowed magnetic numbers.

\[\psi(r,\theta,\varphi)=R(r)Y_\ell^m(\theta,\varphi),\qquad \hat L^2Y_\ell^m=\hbar^2\ell(\ell+1)Y_\ell^m\]
\[\hat L_zY_\ell^m=m\hbar Y_\ell^m,\qquad \ell=0,1,\ldots,\quad -\ell\le m\le\ell.\]
Derivation & detailsSeparation, Legendre functions and angular normalization

Separate the Laplacian

The potential depends only on radius, so the Hamiltonian commutes with \(\hat L^2\) and \(\hat L_z\). Spherical coordinates give

\[\nabla^2=\frac1{r^2}\partial_r(r^2\partial_r)+\frac1{r^2}\Delta_\Omega,\qquad \Delta_\Omega=\frac1{\sin\theta}\partial_\theta(\sin\theta\,\partial_\theta)+\frac1{\sin^2\theta}\partial_\varphi^2.\]

Inserting \(RY\) into the energy equation separates the angular equation \(-\Delta_\Omega Y=\lambda Y\) from the radial equation. Write \(Y=\Theta(\theta)\Phi(\varphi)\). The azimuthal part obeys \(\Phi''=-m^2\Phi\), with solutions \(\exp(\mathrm im\varphi)\). Single-valued spatial states require \(\Phi(\varphi+2\pi)=\Phi(\varphi)\), hence integer \(m\).

An algebraic check on the allowed projections

The same angular spectrum follows from the rotation generators. Their commutators are \([\hat L_i,\hat L_j]=\mathrm i\hbar\epsilon_{ijk}\hat L_k\). Define \(\hat L_\pm=\hat L_x\pm\mathrm i\hat L_y\); these change the \(L_z\) eigenvalue by \(\pm\hbar\) without changing \(L^2\). The squared norm of the raised or lowered state is

\[\|\hat L_\pm|\ell,m\rangle\|^2=\hbar^2[\ell(\ell+1)-m(m\pm1)].\]

A norm cannot be negative, so repeated raising and lowering must terminate at highest and lowest projections. For orbital angular momentum, the single-valued spatial solutions restrict \(\ell\) to nonnegative integers, with endpoints \(m=\pm\ell\). Half-integer angular momentum belongs to spin representations and is not obtained from scalar spherical harmonics. This is a complementary algebraic check on the angular spectrum.

Regularity at both poles

Set \(x=\cos\theta\). The remaining equation is

\[\frac{\mathrm d}{\mathrm dx}\!\left[(1-x^2)\frac{\mathrm d\Theta}{\mathrm dx}\right]+\left[\lambda-\frac{m^2}{1-x^2}\right]\Theta=0.\]

For \(q=|m|\), substitute \(\Theta=(1-x^2)^{q/2}G(x)\). Differentiating and collecting terms gives

\[(1-x^2)G''-2(q+1)xG'+[\lambda-q(q+1)]G=0.\]

Write \(G=\sum_{j=0}^\infty b_jx^j\) and match the coefficient of \(x^j\):

\[b_{j+2}=\frac{(j+q)(j+q+1)-\lambda}{(j+2)(j+1)}\,b_j.\]

The recurrence advances by two powers, so even and odd parity series evolve separately. Regularity at both poles eliminates the singular angular branch; for the ordinary scalar harmonics, the surviving series must terminate. If its highest power is \(d\ge0\), then \(b_{d+2}=0\) requires \(\lambda=(d+q)(d+q+1)\). Define \(\ell=d+q\): the polynomial has degree \(\ell-q\), parity \((-1)^{\ell-q}\), and eigenvalue \(\ell(\ell+1)\). A solution regular at one pole for other eigenvalues is not regular at the other. Thus angular quantization comes from boundary conditions, not a choice to draw a finite number of lobes.

\[P_\ell(x)=\frac1{2^\ell\ell!}\frac{\mathrm d^\ell}{\mathrm dx^\ell}(x^2-1)^\ell,\qquad P_\ell^q(x)=(-1)^q(1-x^2)^{q/2}\frac{\mathrm d^qP_\ell}{\mathrm dx^q}.\]

This defines the Condon–Shortley phase convention. Normalize with \(\mathrm d\Omega=\sin\theta\,\mathrm d\theta\,\mathrm d\varphi\). The azimuthal integral gives \(2\pi\), while the polar integral is \(2(\ell+q)!/[(2\ell+1)(\ell-q)!]\). Therefore

\[Y_\ell^q=\sqrt{\frac{2\ell+1}{4\pi}\frac{(\ell-q)!}{(\ell+q)!}}\,P_\ell^q(\cos\theta)\,\mathrm e^{\mathrm iq\varphi},\qquad Y_\ell^{-q}=(-1)^q(Y_\ell^q)^*.\]

The harmonics satisfy \(\int Y_\ell^{m*}Y_{\ell'}^{m'}\mathrm d\Omega=\delta_{\ell\ell'}\delta_{mm'}\). The labels s, p, d, f, g, h correspond to \(\ell=0,1,2,3,4,5\). The axis defining \(m\) is a coordinate choice until a field or measurement apparatus supplies a physical direction.

08

Boundary conditions quantize the energy

The radial equation has acceptable bound solutions only at discrete energies. The quantization condition emerges when a power series terminates.

\[E_n=-\frac{\mu\kappa^2}{2\hbar^2n^2},\qquad n=1,2,\ldots,\quad 0\le\ell\le n-1\]
\[\rho F''+(2\ell+2-\rho)F'+(n-\ell-1)F=0.\]
Derivation & detailsRadial reduction, asymptotics and the terminating series

Remove the radial volume factor

Use the angular eigenvalue just derived. With \(u(r)=rR(r)\), the equation becomes a one-dimensional problem on \(r>0\):

\[-\frac{\hbar^2}{2\mu}u''+\left[\frac{\hbar^2\ell(\ell+1)}{2\mu r^2}-\frac{\kappa}{r}\right]u=Eu.\]

The centrifugal term is repulsive; it comes from angular kinetic energy. Normalization becomes \(\int_0^\infty|u|^2\mathrm dr=1\). The physical Coulomb Hamiltonian imposes the regular solution at the origin, \(u(0)=0\); merely accepting any locally square-integrable singular expression would be insufficient.

Extract the endpoint behavior

For bound energy \(E<0\), define \(\gamma=\sqrt{-2\mu E}/\hbar\), \(\rho=2\gamma r\) and \(\eta=1/(\gamma a_\mu)\). Then

\[\frac{\mathrm d^2u}{\mathrm d\rho^2}+\left[-\frac14+\frac{\eta}{\rho}-\frac{\ell(\ell+1)}{\rho^2}\right]u=0.\]

Near zero, the dominant powers are \(u\sim\rho^{\ell+1}\) and \(u\sim\rho^{-\ell}\); retain the regular branch. Far away, decay requires \(u\sim\mathrm e^{-\rho/2}\). Put \(u=\rho^{\ell+1}\mathrm e^{-\rho/2}F(\rho)\). Direct differentiation and cancellation leave

\[\rho F''+(2\ell+2-\rho)F'+(\eta-\ell-1)F=0.\]

The decisive recurrence

Substitute \(F=\sum_{j=0}^\infty c_j\rho^j\), collect equal powers and solve for the next coefficient:

\[c_{j+1}=\frac{j+\ell+1-\eta}{(j+1)(j+2\ell+2)}\,c_j.\]

For the regular-at-origin solution, a nonterminating series contains an exponentially growing large-radius component; multiplied by the extracted decay, it still fails normalization. It terminates at \(j=k\) only if \(\eta=k+\ell+1\), where \(k\) is a nonnegative integer. Define that positive integer to be \(n\). Thus \(\gamma=1/(na_\mu)\), \(k=n-\ell-1\), and substituting back into \(E=-\hbar^2\gamma^2/(2\mu)\) gives the spectrum above.

For \(k=0\), \(F\) is constant. For \(k=1\), it is proportional to \(2\ell+2-\rho\). These are the first generalized Laguerre polynomials. No circular orbit or extra angular-momentum rule was needed: regularity, decay and the Schrödinger equation supplied the discrete energies.

09

Normalize the orbital

The radial polynomial and spherical harmonic together give the complete spatial eigenfunction. Normalization converts its square into a quantitative probability density.

\[R_{n\ell}(r)=\sqrt{\left(\frac{2}{na_\mu}\right)^3\frac{(n-\ell-1)!}{2n(n+\ell)!}}\;\mathrm e^{-\rho/2}\rho^\ell L_{n-\ell-1}^{2\ell+1}(\rho),\quad \rho=\frac{2r}{na_\mu}\]
\[\Psi_{n\ell m}=R_{n\ell}Y_\ell^m\mathrm e^{-\mathrm iE_nt/\hbar},\qquad \int|\Psi_{n\ell m}|^2\mathrm d^3r=1.\]
Derivation & detailsLaguerre normalization and explicit 1s and 2p examples

Fix the remaining constant

Let \(k=n-\ell-1\), \(\alpha=2\ell+1\), and \(R=C\mathrm e^{-\rho/2}\rho^\ell L_k^\alpha\). Angular normalization is already one, so only \(\int|R|^2r^2\mathrm dr=1\) remains. Since \(r^2\mathrm dr=(na_\mu/2)^3\rho^2\mathrm d\rho\), the needed identity is

\[\int_0^\infty\mathrm e^{-\rho}\rho^{\alpha+1}\left[L_k^\alpha(\rho)\right]^2\mathrm d\rho=(2k+\alpha+1)\frac{\Gamma(k+\alpha+1)}{k!}.\]

It follows by multiplying the three-term recurrence for \(\rho L_k^\alpha\) by \(L_k^\alpha\), integrating with weight \(\mathrm e^{-\rho}\rho^\alpha\), and using orthogonality to remove the neighboring degrees. Here \(2k+\alpha+1=2n\) and \(\Gamma(k+\alpha+1)=(n+\ell)!\). Solving for \(C\) produces the normalization in the main equation.

Orthogonality and completeness

Distinct eigenvalues of a self-adjoint Hamiltonian have orthogonal eigenstates: evaluating \(\langle a|\hat H|b\rangle\) from the left and right gives \((E_a-E_b)\langle a|b\rangle=0\). At equal energy, the angular eigenvalues and harmonic orthogonality distinguish the usual basis states. The normalized bound functions therefore form a countable orthonormal family.

They do not alone span every possible electron state. Positive-energy Coulomb scattering states form a continuum and are needed for ionization, incoming electrons and general wavepackets. An arbitrary state requires both a sum over bound states and an integral over continuum energies. Restricting the atlas to six bound shells is a deliberate finite selection from that larger space, not a statement that hydrogen has only six shells or that all physical states resemble one displayed orbital.

Check the ground state

\[R_{10}=2a_\mu^{-3/2}\mathrm e^{-r/a_\mu},\quad Y_0^0=\frac1{\sqrt{4\pi}},\quad\psi_{100}=\frac{\mathrm e^{-r/a_\mu}}{\sqrt{\pi a_\mu^3}}.\]

Integrating its square gives \((4/a_\mu^3)\int_0^\infty r^2\mathrm e^{-2r/a_\mu}\mathrm dr=1\), because \(\int_0^\infty r^2\mathrm e^{-br}\mathrm dr=2/b^3\). This also checks the units: \(\psi\) has dimension length to the power \(-3/2\), and density has dimension length to the power \(-3\).

The 2p, \(m=0\) density is proportional to \(r^2\mathrm e^{-r/a_\mu}\cos^2\theta\): two lobes separated by the equatorial nodal plane. Read the probability, nodes and degeneracies

Local density, probability in a radial shell and the visible outline are different quantities. Quantum numbers count states and nodes; they do not specify classical paths.

\[P_r(r)=r^2|R_{n\ell}(r)|^2,\qquad N_{\mathrm{radial}}=n-\ell-1,\qquad g_n=\sum_{\ell=0}^{n-1}(2\ell+1)=n^2\]
\[\langle r\rangle=\frac{a_\mu}{2}\left[3n^2-\ell(\ell+1)\right],\qquad \langle r^{-1}\rangle=\frac1{a_\mu n^2}.\]
Derivation & detailsVolume measure, physical scales, nodal structure and state counting

A shell contains more volume at larger radius

The spherical volume element is \(r^2\mathrm dr\,\mathrm d\Omega\). Integrating \(|RY|^2\) over direction therefore gives \(P_r\), with \(P_r\mathrm dr\) the probability between \(r\) and \(r+\mathrm dr\). For 1s, \(P_r=4r^2a_\mu^{-3}\mathrm e^{-2r/a_\mu}\). Differentiating its logarithm gives \(2/r-2/a_\mu=0\), so its maximum is at \(a_\mu\), even though the local density is greatest at the nucleus.

Moments come from \(\langle r^s\rangle=\int_0^\infty r^{s+2}|R|^2\mathrm dr\). Substituting the Laguerre solution and using recurrence relations yields the two examples above. The virial theorem provides a useful independent check: for \(V=-\kappa/r\), \(2\langle T\rangle=-\langle V\rangle\), so \(E=\langle V\rangle/2=-\kappa\langle1/r\rangle/2\).

Derive the virial check by changing scale

Take a normalized stationary bound state and the equally normalized trial family \(\psi_\lambda(\mathbf r)=\lambda^{3/2}\psi(\lambda\mathbf r)\). A change of integration variable shows that kinetic energy scales as \(\lambda^2\), while the Coulomb potential energy scales as \(\lambda\). Hence \(E(\lambda)=\lambda^2\langle T\rangle+\lambda\langle V\rangle\). The expectation is stationary at the exact eigenstate, so its derivative at \(\lambda=1\) vanishes. This gives \(2\langle T\rangle+\langle V\rangle=0\).

Consequently the binding energy is not entirely potential energy: \(\langle T\rangle=-E>0\) and \(\langle V\rangle=2E<0\). The electron's nonzero kinetic energy is compatible with a time-independent density. It describes the momentum distribution of the state and should not be read as the speed of a point following the cloud's outline.

What counts as a node?

The degree-\(k\) Laguerre polynomial has \(k\) positive radial roots. They form spherical nodal surfaces; the origin and the infinite tail are not counted. For complex harmonics, \(P_\ell^{|m|}\) supplies \(\ell-|m|\) polar-angle zeros away from the poles. A real harmonic adds \(|m|\) azimuthal nodal planes. Thus the familiar count of \(\ell\) angular nodal surfaces applies to the usual real orbital basis; complex states have different nodal geometry. For 6f, radial nodes number \(6-3-1=2\).

Count states without counting pictures twice

At fixed \(n\), summing \(2\ell+1\) magnetic states over \(\ell=0\) to \(n-1\) gives \(n^2\) spatial states. This degeneracy is special to the ideal Coulomb problem; a general central potential can depend on both \(n\) and \(\ell\). Including electron spin doubles the count to \(2n^2\), but spin is absent from this atlas.

Through \(n=6\), there are \(\sum n^2=91\) signed spatial eigenstates. Since opposite signs of complex \(m\) share a density, keeping \(|m|=0,\ldots,\ell\) gives \(\sum_{n=1}^6 n(n+1)/2=56\) density shapes. This is why the atlas can show fewer silhouettes than quantum states.

The equality of energies for different angular momenta is sometimes called accidental degeneracy, but it reflects an additional symmetry of the inverse-distance potential. The classical Kepler problem has a conserved Runge–Lenz vector; its quantum counterpart relates states of different angular momentum within one shell. Perturbations that do not preserve that symmetry generally split the shell. Rotational symmetry alone explains equality among magnetic projections, whereas the larger Coulomb symmetry explains equality across different orbital families at fixed principal number.

No bound orbital has a sharp outer boundary. Exponential tails extend arbitrarily far. A rendered radius, cutoff or opacity contour is a display choice, not the edge of the atom.

Conservation, circulation and real orbitals

The Schrödinger equation conserves probability locally. Complex magnetic states can carry stationary circulation; real combinations have different density shapes and zero stationary current.

\[\partial_t\rho+\nabla\cdot\mathbf j=0,\qquad\rho=|\Psi|^2,\qquad\mathbf j=\frac{\hbar}{\mu}\operatorname{Im}(\Psi^*\nabla\Psi)\]
\[\mathbf v=\frac{\mathbf j}{\rho}=\frac{m\hbar}{\mu r\sin\theta}\,\hat{\boldsymbol\varphi},\qquad \dot\varphi=\frac{m\hbar}{\mu r^2\sin^2\theta}.\]
Derivation & detailsContinuity equation, phase gradients and basis changes

Derive local conservation

Multiply the Schrödinger equation by \(\Psi^*\), multiply its complex conjugate by \(\Psi\), and subtract. For a real scalar potential, the potential terms cancel. Using the product rule gives

\[\partial_t|\Psi|^2=\frac{\mathrm i\hbar}{2\mu}\left(\Psi^*\nabla^2\Psi-\Psi\nabla^2\Psi^*\right)=-\nabla\cdot\left[\frac{\hbar}{2\mu\mathrm i}(\Psi^*\nabla\Psi-\Psi\nabla\Psi^*)\right].\]

Integrating over a volume converts the divergence into outward flux through its boundary. With vanishing flux at infinity, total probability stays one. This expression assumes no vector potential; magnetic fields require the gauge-covariant current.

Phase produces the circulating field

Write \(\Psi=\sqrt\rho\,\mathrm e^{\mathrm iS/\hbar}\). The derivative of the real amplitude contributes no imaginary part, leaving \(\mathbf j=\rho\nabla S/\mu\). For \(Y_\ell^m\propto\mathrm e^{\mathrm im\varphi}\), the spatial phase is \(S=m\hbar\varphi\), and \(\nabla\varphi=\hat{\boldsymbol\varphi}/(r\sin\theta)\). This gives the circulation equations above wherever \(\rho>0\).

Changing \(m\) to \(-m\) conjugates the angular function up to a constant phase: the density stays the same and the current reverses. At nodes, \(\mathbf j/\rho\) is undefined; the viewer regularizes numerical motion near low-density regions. Tracers visualize this field. They do not establish measured electron trajectories, and their speed is rescaled for human viewing.

Construct the real basis

For \(q>0\), form orthonormal combinations within the degenerate \(\pm q\) pair:

\[Y_{\ell q}^{(c)}=\frac{Y_\ell^q+(-1)^qY_\ell^{-q}}{\sqrt2}=\sqrt2\,\operatorname{Re}Y_\ell^q,\qquad Y_{\ell q}^{(s)}=\frac{Y_\ell^q-(-1)^qY_\ell^{-q}}{\mathrm i\sqrt2}=\sqrt2\,\operatorname{Im}Y_\ell^q.\]

These contain \(\cos(q\varphi)\) and \(\sin(q\varphi)\); their density gains directional lobes. The transformation is unitary, so the real functions span the same state space. They remain eigenstates of \(\hat H\) and \(\hat L^2\), but for \(q>0\) they are not eigenstates of \(\hat L_z\). Their magnetic label identifies which real combination is being displayed, not a definite signed \(L_z\) measurement.

A stationary real spatial function has \(\operatorname{Im}(\psi\nabla\psi)=0\); its global time phase cancels from the current. Its mean \(L_z\) is zero, although a measurement can return \(\pm q\hbar\). The \(m=0\) complex harmonic is already real. These facts explain why the site's flow control is meaningful in Complex mode and hidden in Real mode.

Selection rules, polarization and coupling

Electric-dipole selection rules identify allowed pairs. Their matrix elements determine coupling strength; a nonzero coupling is not a transition probability.

\[L=\ell\pm1,\qquad q=M-m\in\{-1,0,+1\},\qquad |m|\le\ell,\quad |M|\le L.\]
\[S_c=\left|I_{a\ell,bL}\right|^2\left|\mathcal A_c\right|^2,\qquad s_c=\frac{S_c}{\max_{c'\in\mathcal C_{ab}}S_{c'}}.\]
Derivation & detailsSelection rules, angular overlap and strength

Why a matrix element appears at all

Let \(\hat H=\hat H_0+\hat V(t)\), with the unperturbed eigenstates already derived. Expand the evolving state in that complete basis, including continuum states when needed. Substitution into Schrödinger’s equation and projection onto \(\langle f|\) gives

\[|\Psi(t)\rangle=\sum_i c_i(t)\mathrm e^{-\mathrm iE_it/\hbar}|i\rangle,\qquad \mathrm i\hbar\dot c_f=\sum_i V_{fi}(t)\mathrm e^{\mathrm i\omega_{fi}t}c_i,\quad\omega_{fi}=\frac{E_f-E_i}{\hbar}.\]

In first-order perturbation theory the initial state is barely depleted, so \(c_i\simeq1\) and the other starting amplitudes vanish. Integrating once yields

\[c_f^{(1)}(t)=-\frac{\mathrm i}{\hbar}\int_0^t V_{fi}(t')\mathrm e^{\mathrm i\omega_{fi}t'}\,\mathrm dt'.\]

For the resonant Fourier component \(V_{fi}=v_{fi}\mathrm e^{-\mathrm i\omega t}\), define detuning \(\Delta=\omega_{fi}-\omega\). Then

\[|c_f^{(1)}(t)|^2=\frac{|v_{fi}|^2t^2}{\hbar^2}\operatorname{sinc}^2\!\left(\frac{\Delta t}{2}\right),\qquad \operatorname{sinc}x=\frac{\sin x}{x}.\]

The frequency dependence selects resonance; the spatial matrix element selects which states couple. This approximation requires the predicted probability to remain small. It cannot describe repeated complete population transfer, for which the two-state equations in the next topic must be solved without freezing the initial amplitude.

Why the dipole rules follow

In the long-wavelength approximation, the electron’s dipole is \(\hat{\mathbf d}=-e\mathbf r\), with interaction \(\hat H'= -\hat{\mathbf d}\cdot\mathbf E(t)\). Multiplying a position-operator component by \(-e\) gives the corresponding dipole component.

The azimuthal part of the angular overlap contains \(\mathrm e^{\mathrm i(m+q-M)\varphi}\). Its integral over a full turn is zero unless \(M=m+q\). The rank-one dipole has \(q=0,\pm1\), giving \(|\Delta m|\le1\).

\[\int_0^{2\pi}\mathrm e^{\mathrm i(m+q-M)\varphi}\,\mathrm d\varphi=2\pi\,\delta_{M,m+q}.\]

Angular-momentum addition allows \(L=\ell-1,\ell,\ell+1\). The odd parity of \(\mathbf r\) requires opposite parities for the two states, excluding \(L=\ell\) and leaving \(\Delta\ell=\pm1\). Separating radial and angular factors then turns the squared dipole amplitude into the strength \(S_c\) in the main equation.

Dipole components and polarization

\[r_0=z,\qquad r_{\pm1}=\mp\frac{x\pm\mathrm i y}{\sqrt2}=r\sqrt{\frac{4\pi}{3}}\,Y_1^{\pm1},\qquad r_0=r\sqrt{\frac{4\pi}{3}}\,Y_1^0.\]

Write the lower state as \(|a,\ell,m\rangle\) and the upper state as \(|b,L,M\rangle\). The channel label is always \(q=M-m\): the change in the atom's magnetic quantum number during absorption. Nonzero coupling obeys the selection rules shown in the main equation.

Parity changes because the dipole operator is odd. There is no additional electric-dipole rule restricting \(\Delta n\). Same-shell changes have zero photon energy in this model and are not finite-wavelength spectral lines.

The radial functions are normalized as in topic 09. The angular integral is a Gaunt coefficient; equivalently, using Wigner \(3j\) symbols,

\[\mathcal A_{\ell m,L M}^{(q)}=(-1)^M\sqrt{(2L+1)(2\ell+1)}\begin{pmatrix}L&1&\ell\\0&0&0\end{pmatrix}\begin{pmatrix}L&1&\ell\\-M&q&m\end{pmatrix}.\]

Here \(\int_0^\infty r^k\mathrm e^{-\beta r}\,\mathrm dr=k!/\beta^{k+1}\). The angular factor is \(1/\sqrt3\), giving the resolved \(z\)-component

\[\langle2p,0|z|1s,0\rangle=\frac{256}{243\sqrt2}\,a_0,\qquad S=\frac{32768}{59049}\,a_0^2\simeq0.55493\,a_0^2.\]

The other two allowed magnetic channels have the same strength in their corresponding spherical components. This analytic result is a useful check on numerical quadrature; the code computes each matrix element from radial and angular factors, independently of the rendering.

What dipole strength means

The readout measures the squared spatial dipole matrix element. The relative value is normalized against the strongest magnetic channel on the same wavelength, including all subshells and all three polarization components.

\(S_c\) has units of \(a_0^2\); multiplying it by \(e^2\) gives the squared electric-dipole moment. Filters do not change the denominator. A relative value of one identifies the strongest channel at that wavelength; it is not a 100% transition probability.

Observed line brightness also depends on the number of atoms in each state, the illuminating field, polarization, frequency, decay and observation geometry. Spontaneous-emission rates scale as \(\omega^3\) times dipole strength; emitted power also includes photon energy and upper-state population. Summing channels requires a specified population model. The spectrum therefore presents coupling strengths, not predicted measured intensities.

This free-space electric-dipole spontaneous-emission formula refers to a specified upper and lower state, with emitted directions and polarizations summed and the vector dipole norm used. A lifetime requires summing rates into all accessible lower states. Its derivation also needs the quantized radiation field; a prescribed classical resonant drive alone does not generate spontaneous decay. The atlas does not use this formula to time its animation.

Driven transitions and what the animation shows

A resonant drive exchanges population between an isolated pair of states. The animation keeps that slow exchange and averages over the much faster optical oscillation.

\[P_u(t)=\sin^2\!\left(\frac{\Omega t}{2}\right),\qquad P_l(t)=1-P_u(t).\]
\[\bar\rho(\mathbf r,t)=[1-P_u(t)]|\psi_l(\mathbf r)|^2+P_u(t)|\psi_u(\mathbf r)|^2\]
Derivation & detailsTwo-state solution, optical averaging and scope

State the approximations before solving

Retain two levels coupled by a nearly resonant classical field. Assume the coupling is weak compared with the optical frequency, the drive has a stable phase, other resonances are sufficiently separated or suppressed, and decay is negligible over the interval. Transform away the rapid unperturbed phases and discard the counter-rotating terms oscillating near twice the optical frequency. This is the rotating-wave approximation, not an identity for arbitrary fields.

\[\hat H_{\mathrm{rot}}=\frac{\hbar}{2}\begin{pmatrix}-\Delta&\Omega\\\Omega&\Delta\end{pmatrix},\qquad \Omega_{\mathrm R}=\sqrt{\Omega^2+\Delta^2},\qquad \Delta=\omega_{ul}-\omega.\]

Choosing the drive phase makes \(\Omega\) real and nonnegative. Squaring this matrix gives \(\hat H_{\mathrm{rot}}^2=\hbar^2\Omega_{\mathrm R}^2\mathbf1/4\), so the exponential evolution operator follows by collecting even and odd powers:

\[\mathrm e^{-\mathrm i\hat H_{\mathrm{rot}}t/\hbar}=\mathbf1\cos\!\left(\frac{\Omega_{\mathrm R}t}{2}\right)-\frac{2\mathrm i\hat H_{\mathrm{rot}}}{\hbar\Omega_{\mathrm R}}\sin\!\left(\frac{\Omega_{\mathrm R}t}{2}\right).\]

Starting in the lower state gives \(P_u=(\Omega^2/\Omega_{\mathrm R}^2)\sin^2(\Omega_{\mathrm R}t/2)\). Detuning reduces the maximum transfer. The display uses the resonant case \(\Delta=0\), for which complete exchange is possible in this idealized two-level model.

Solving the resonant two-state problem

Choose the drive phase so the resonant rotating-frame coupling is real. With the atom initially in the lower state, the effective equations are

\[\mathrm i\frac{\mathrm d}{\mathrm dt}\begin{pmatrix}c_l\\c_u\end{pmatrix}=\frac\Omega2\begin{pmatrix}0&1\\1&0\end{pmatrix}\begin{pmatrix}c_l\\c_u\end{pmatrix},\qquad\begin{pmatrix}c_l(0)\\c_u(0)\end{pmatrix}=\begin{pmatrix}1\\0\end{pmatrix}.\]

Differentiating once more gives \(\ddot c_l=-(\Omega/2)^2c_l\). Thus \(c_l=\cos(\Omega t/2)\), \(c_u=-\mathrm i\sin(\Omega t/2)\), and taking squared magnitudes gives the population cycle. Its period is \(2\pi/\Omega\), distinct from the optical period \(2\pi/\omega\).

Expanding the coherent density gives two population terms and an interference term. Averaging over many optical cycles removes the rapidly varying cross term, leaving the displayed mixture while preserving total probability.

Driving field and visual time

For a monochromatic field \(\mathbf E(t)=\operatorname{Re}[\boldsymbol{\mathcal E}\,\mathrm e^{-\mathrm i\omega t}]\), define \(\Omega=|\langle u|\hat{\mathbf d}\cdot\boldsymbol{\mathcal E}|l\rangle|/\hbar\). Thus physical Rabi rates depend on field amplitude, polarization and the channel's dipole matrix element. With one matched field component held fixed, \(\Omega\) scales as \(\sqrt{S_c}\).

The display defaults to a 2.5-second cycle

Every preset starts at 1×, giving a 2.5-second cycle. The transition-speed control scales the visual cycle in five steps: 1/4×, 1/2×, 1×, 2× and 4× (10 to 0.625 seconds), independently of circulation speed. This visual timing makes different shapes easy to compare and does not predict physical Rabi times or scale playback by dipole strength. Each descriptor’s border brightens toward white as its population grows. The rising upper-state population illustrates absorption; the falling population illustrates stimulated emission. The repeating animation is not repeated spontaneous decay.

The coherent wavefunction contains two energy-dependent phases:

\[\Psi(\mathbf r,t)=c_l(t)\psi_l(\mathbf r)\mathrm e^{-\mathrm iE_lt/\hbar}+c_u(t)\psi_u(\mathbf r)\mathrm e^{-\mathrm iE_ut/\hbar}.\]

Its density has an interference term oscillating at the optical difference frequency. The transition cloud averages over that fast oscillation while retaining the slow populations:

\[\begin{aligned}|\Psi|^2={}&|c_l|^2|\psi_l|^2+|c_u|^2|\psi_u|^2\\&+2\operatorname{Re}\!\left[c_l^*c_u\psi_l^*\psi_u\,\mathrm e^{-\mathrm i(E_u-E_l)t/\hbar}\right],\\[0.7em]\bar\rho(\mathbf r,t)={}&\bigl[1-P_u(t)\bigr]|\psi_l(\mathbf r)|^2+P_u(t)|\psi_u(\mathbf r)|^2.\end{aligned}\]

The averaging assumes \(\omega_{ul}^{-1}\ll\tau\ll\Omega^{-1}\): an interval long compared with the optical period and short compared with the population cycle. The two normalized distributions share one physical coordinate scale; a common visual exposure is applied after mixing. Both probabilities add to one, and the integrated density remains one.

Every selected channel uses the complex magnetic basis \(|n,\ell,m\rangle\). Opposite signs of \(m\) have identical density, so some different channels look the same in this averaged view even though their phase, current or polarization coupling differs. The state labels and dipole-strength readout retain that distinction. The atlas's real orbitals are superpositions of the complex states, not additional channels to be added to the count.

Two-state scope

With degenerate magnetic sublevels, a real applied field can couple several states simultaneously. This view isolates one matrix element at a time; a wavelength alone does not prepare a unique channel. It omits multilevel coherent dynamics, spontaneous decay, decoherence, saturation in an ensemble and line broadening. Electric-quadrupole, magnetic-dipole, multiphoton and ionization processes are outside this electric-dipole, bound-state model. A dipole-forbidden transition can still occur by another mechanism.

Complex states and real-basis previews

Complex mode displays the two magnetic eigenstates named by the selected channel. Real mode replaces each endpoint by its normalized real counterpart from topic 11: the positive index selects a cosine combination, the negative index a sine combination. This is a preview of different basis shapes in the same energy shells, not a claim that their coupling equals the selected complex channel's coupling.

The wavelength is unchanged because these combinations have the same shell energies. The displayed selection rules, polarization and strength continue to refer to the named complex states. A physical transition calculation between real combinations would transform the full matrix of dipole amplitudes before taking its squared magnitude:

\[D_{\mathrm{real}}^{(q)}=U_u^\dagger D_{\mathrm{complex}}^{(q)}U_l.\]

Insert the expansions of both real endpoints into the complex-basis matrix element; the bra contributes the conjugate-transpose matrix, while the ket contributes the matrix itself. The columns of \(U_l\) and \(U_u\) contain the real basis vectors expressed in their complex magnetic bases. Amplitudes must be transformed before taking squared magnitudes because different terms can interfere. The signed index in a real preview is not a definite value of angular momentum along \(z\). Real combinations add no independent states to the catalog.

Component phase is a color convention

After optical averaging, the density is a population-weighted sum. This averaged representation has no unique single wavefunction phase to color; it does not assert that the underlying driven state has physically decohered. The cloud therefore colors each component's phase separately, then blends its colors according to local density. With \(p_l=1-P\), \(p_u=P\), \(\rho_k=|\psi_k|^2\), and a cyclic color map \(F\), the unshaded volume color is

\[\mathbf C(\mathbf r)=\frac{p_l\rho_l(\mathbf r)F\!\bigl(\phi_l(\mathbf r)\bigr)+p_u\rho_u(\mathbf r)F\!\bigl(\phi_u(\mathbf r)\bigr)}{p_l\rho_l(\mathbf r)+p_u\rho_u(\mathbf r)},\qquad\bar\rho(\mathbf r)>0.\]

Here \(\phi_k\) is the phase of one component in the display's chosen convention; each component particle carries its own phase color. Zero-density regions contribute no color. Phase wraps at \(-\pi\) and \(+\pi\); a noncyclic palette can have a visible color seam there. Blended colors are not the phase of a coherent superposition and do not restore the interference term omitted in this topic. Changing a component's arbitrary overall phase changes its colors without changing its density or coupling strength.

Circulation is separate from population transfer

The tracers sample each endpoint distribution and circle according to that component's stationary current. Their population weights change with the cycle. They illustrate the component circulation field

\[\mathbf j_{\mathrm{circ}}=(1-P)\mathbf j_l+P\mathbf j_u,\qquad\mathbf j_k=\frac{\hbar}{m_e}\operatorname{Im}\!\left(\psi_k^*\nabla\psi_k\right).\]

Each stationary complex component follows the azimuthal flow in topic 11. Opposite magnetic signs circulate in opposite directions. A stationary real component, or a component with \(m=0\), has zero circulation. The moving samples do not travel from one orbital to the other.

This overlay does not solve the full local probability transport of the driven atom. The distinction can be seen directly: each stationary current is divergence-free, whereas the chosen population interpolation changes the local density, giving

\[\frac{\partial\bar\rho}{\partial t}+\nabla\!\cdot\mathbf j_{\mathrm{circ}}=\dot P\left(\rho_u-\rho_l\right).\]

The right-hand side integrates to zero because both endpoint densities are normalized. It records the change of the displayed component weights; it is not creation or loss of an electron. The full driven quantum evolution conserves probability locally, but its transfer current and fast coherences are outside this averaged visualization. The circulation overlay alone must not be interpreted as that full current.

Visible spectral colors are approximate; ultraviolet and infrared lines use blue interface markers, not visible photon colors. From a wavefunction to pixels

The browser evaluates known hydrogen solutions, samples their probability distributions, and projects them with WebGL 2. It does not solve a many-body problem or integrate Schrödinger’s equation on a three-dimensional grid each frame. The physics supplies the distribution; the rendering algorithm supplies its visible appearance.

Algorithms & implementationSampling, shaders, numerical accuracy and frame budgets

1. Choose the mathematical state

The implemented domain is the spin-free, fixed-proton Coulomb model with shells \(1\le n\le6\). Distances are expressed in Bohr radii. The selected \(n,\ell,m\), basis and display settings become shader uniforms. Density mode uses \(|\psi|^2\); real-basis mode multiplies the angular amplitude by the appropriate normalized sine or cosine combination. The implementation includes the radial and angular normalization factors, so these are normalized analytic eigenfunctions before the finite viewing region and display mapping are applied.

Generalized Laguerre and associated Legendre polynomials are evaluated with recurrences rather than a separate hard-coded formula for every orbital. For example, the radial evaluator uses

\[L_0^\alpha(x)=1,\quad L_1^\alpha(x)=1+\alpha-x,\quad L_j^\alpha(x)=\frac{(2j-1+\alpha-x)L_{j-1}^\alpha(x)-(j-1+\alpha)L_{j-2}^\alpha(x)}{j}.\]

For the angular factor, the initial \(P_m^m\) contains the Condon–Shortley sign, \(P_{m+1}^m=(2m+1)cP_m^m\), and the upward recurrence generates the required degree. With only six shells, these polynomials have modest degree. CPU calculations prepare distributions and reference values; GLSL shaders repeat the relevant evaluation for visible samples.

2. Bound an infinite distribution for display

A bound-state wavefunction has an exponential tail, not a sharp outer surface. The orbital viewer uses a finite radius \(r_{\max}/a_0=2.15n^2+3\). A transition uses the larger endpoint shell in that expression, placing both orbitals in one shared coordinate system. This is a practical display bound, not a stated 90% or 99% probability surface. For individual orbitals through six shells, this cutoff omits about 0.11%–3.95% of the radial probability, depending on the state. These fractions follow by integrating the normalized radial density beyond the cutoff. Cloud opacity also fades near its outer edge; particle sampling is normalized over the retained region. The particle distribution is therefore the original distribution conditioned on that region, and the cropped tail is a display approximation, not physical confinement.

Brightness is also rescaled. The renderer estimates peaks and uses a reference related to the radial-probability maximum to keep outer lobes visible. The default density map runs from dark blue to white, followed by lighting and tone mapping. The shared color-map setting substitutes a different display ramp in both animations, legends and thumbnail catalogs without changing any physical density or coupling. Named multicolor ramps use sampled color stops with linear interpolation. Density and phase use the same selected palette, including the default Blue. Phase maps one complete cycle onto that ramp; noncyclic ramps can have a seam at the wrap point. Thus a white pixel does not mean probability one, and equally bright pixels in different images need not represent equal absolute density. Atlas thumbnails additionally have independent spatial and brightness scales.

3. Generate samples with inverse cumulative distributions

For a complex eigenstate, the density separates into a radial factor, a polar factor and a uniform azimuth. The spherical Jacobian matters: sampling radius uniformly would put far too many samples in the wrong places. The code tabulates cumulative sums proportional to \(r^2|R|^2\) and \(|P_\ell^{|m|}(c)|^2\), normalizes each cumulative array to one, and draws independent uniform numbers.

\[F_r(r)=\frac{\int_0^r s^2|R(s)|^2\,\mathrm ds}{\int_0^{r_{\max}}s^2|R(s)|^2\,\mathrm ds},\qquad r=F_r^{-1}(u_r),\quad c=F_c^{-1}(u_c),\quad\varphi=2\pi u_\varphi.\]

Binary search locates each uniform number in its cumulative table; linear interpolation supplies a coordinate between adjacent entries. The single-orbital implementation uses 1,500 radial and 800 polar cumulative entries, formed with uniform-grid sums. The transition implementation uses 2,048 radial and 1,025 polar entries with trapezoidal accumulation. These are numerical approximations to the integrals, not symbolic inverses. A deterministic pseudorandom generator makes the point pattern reproducible instead of replacing it with fresh noise every frame.

Real orbitals with nonzero \(m\) require a nonuniform azimuth. Rejection sampling accepts an azimuth with probability \(\cos^2(m\varphi)\) or \(\sin^2(|m|\varphi)\), depending on the real-basis convention. The orbital renderer bounds the retry loop at 80 attempts; the transition renderer continues until acceptance. Each stored sample contains normalized radius, \(\cos\theta\), initial azimuth and a display-size seed. Four 32-bit floats per sample mean 200,000 seeds occupy approximately 3.2 MB, before GPU copies, cached states and framebuffers. The transition retains a seed buffer for each endpoint, even though the active count is shared between them.

4. Cloud: integrate through the volume

Additional color metrics. Probability current uses the magnitude of j = ρv; in a complex hydrogen eigenstate the azimuthal speed is |m|/r_perp in atomic units. Angular speed is |m|/r_perp². Both vanish for a stationary real-basis orbital. A tiny axis cutoff prevents division by zero. For transitions the component currents are population-weighted before taking their magnitude, so opposing currents can cancel. These are the averaged component currents, not a calculated transfer current. Radial probability uses r²|R_nl(r)|², blended by population in Spectra. Density gradient uses centered finite differences of physical density, with a step of 0.002 times the rendering radius. It requires extra density evaluations and can be slower in Cloud mode.

Transition-only metrics. State contribution is Pρ_upper / [(1−P)ρ_lower + Pρ_upper], with a fixed 0–1 range and no log transform. Density change is (dP/dt)(ρ_upper−ρ_lower), per display second of the eased animation. It vanishes at endpoints and when paused, and changes sign between absorption and emission. Its fixed symmetric limits use a density-weighted percentile of the absolute endpoint-density difference multiplied by the maximum population slope at the selected transition speed. Zero is at the midpoint of the selected palette. Changing the color metric preserves the selected colormap. Log scaling is disabled for this signed metric. Pair-card thumbnails retain ordinary density for both transition-only modes; the four other new metrics also apply to individual thumbnails. None of these color choices alters opacity or particle sampling.

Density-weighted color ranges. Scalar color limits use the 1st and 99th percentiles weighted by probability density and spherical volume. Deterministic quadrature samples each endpoint; transition endpoints contribute equal total probability mass. Limits are cached by quantum numbers, basis and metric, independent of population, camera, cuts and animation speed. Linear color maps clamp to these limits. Log mode uses 1 − log(x/x_max) / log(x_min/x_max), after clamping. Low values map to 0 and high values to 1, matching the direction of the linear color scale. If the lower percentile is zero, a floor of one millionth of the upper limit prevents log(0); a constant-valued field maps to the first color. Phase keeps its fixed angular range with no logarithmic transform. Thumbnails use density-weighted percentiles of their own cross sections. These are display ranges; they do not alter density, opacity, sampling or coupling strength.

Color by. Probability density colors the display-normalized density. Wavefunction magnitude colors its square root; in the optical-cycle-averaged transition this is the square root of the population-weighted density, not the magnitude of a coherent superposition. Distance from nucleus colors the radius divided by the rendering extent (the same extent for both states of a pair). Cylindrical radius colors sqrt(x² + y²), the distance from the orbital z axis divided by the rendering extent. It is independent of z and camera rotation and uses the same density-weighted limits and optional log scaling. Polar angle colors the angle from the orbital positive z axis, from 0 to 180 degrees. At the origin the angle is assigned zero. These choices change only color, not opacity, particle sampling, or transition strength. Thumbnail phase is a time-zero complex-state cross section; thumbnail density and radius scales remain independent for each orbital.

Dragging can subsequently rotate the camera. Cut shape and viewing direction are retained for each page and rendering style. The opacity coefficient is an artistic transfer function, roughly proportional to the displayed density raised to \(0.78\), multiplied by intensity and a scale constant. 5. Granular: draw analytic sphere impostors

Granular draws small shaded beads at sampled positions. These are visual probability samples, not separate electrons and not literal finite-sized electron balls. Instead of drawing a many-triangle mesh for every bead, the shader draws a small screen-facing footprint and analytically intersects the viewing ray with a sphere. The intersection supplies its silhouette, normal, lighting and fragment depth. Depth testing gives opaque overlap and a strong sense of structure.

The Orbits implementation uses instanced quads, six vertices per bead. Spectra can use one point vertex per bead when the hardware’s point-size range can safely contain the projected footprint; it falls back to instanced quads for very large or near-camera projections. Spectra’s Granular mode skips the volume ray-march entirely, so its main rendering cost is the active beads and their covered pixels. Orbits retains a faint volume background at 5.5% strength; in an unchanged density view that background can be cached while the beads continue moving. Camera or phase changes can require it to be recomputed.

6. Advance an illustrative current, not an electron trajectory

For a stationary complex eigenstate, the current field implies an azimuthal angular speed proportional to \(m/(r^2\sin^2\theta)\). Radius and polar angle remain fixed. The renderer can therefore update each sample’s azimuth directly from its stored seed and accumulated display time:

\[\varphi(t_{\mathrm{vis}})=\varphi_0+\omega_{\mathrm{vis}}t_{\mathrm{vis}},\qquad \omega_{\mathrm{vis}}\propto\frac{m}{\max(\varepsilon,r^2\sin^2\theta)}.\]

A small denominator floor avoids numerical explosions near the axis, and shell-dependent scale factors make circulation visible. This is not a numerical Newtonian orbit integration or a physically calibrated clock. A stationary real orbital, or \(m=0\), has no such circulation. Frame timestamps advance display time; the Spectra renderer caps its per-draw time increment at 0.1 seconds after a stall, whereas the Orbits renderer uses its elapsed visible-frame interval. Hidden views suspend rendering or reset timing. The phase palette has its own deliberately slowed display convention; its animation should not be used to infer actual atomic frequencies.

7. Combine two endpoint distributions

The transition controller first computes \(p=\sin^2(\pi s)\), where \(s\) is the adjustable cycle fraction, then applies the display easing \(P=6p^5-15p^4+10p^3\). This keeps the same cycle length while lingering near each pure state and moving more quickly through the middle. The easing is a viewing aid, not a change to the physical Rabi solution. Cloud evaluates \((1-P)|\psi_l|^2+P|\psi_u|^2\). For speed, the CPU tabulates both signed radial amplitudes in a 2,048-entry, two-channel floating-point texture. The shader interpolates adjacent entries manually and evaluates their angular factors. Keeping amplitudes signed preserves radial phase changes; squaring them gives density.

Granular rounds the lower component’s share to an integer and assigns the rest of the active budget to the upper component. The samples retain their own endpoint circulation and phase colors. They do not migrate along a computed path between orbitals. Optical-frequency interference and the full driven transfer current are outside this averaged animation. Equal component weights imply equal expected sample shares, not equal brightness in every projected region.

8. Compute transition strengths separately

The renderer does not estimate dipole strength from pixels or count particles. The transition module enumerates the electric-dipole-allowed channels, evaluates exact algebraic angular factors, and numerically integrates the radial matrix element \(\int R_uR_l r^3\,\mathrm dr\). Composite Simpson quadrature starts at 1,024 intervals and doubles up to 16,384, stopping when successive values differ by at most \(2\times10^{-11}|I|+10^{-12}\) in atomic units.

The integration endpoint is \(80/(1/n_l+1/n_u)\) Bohr radii, so the product’s exponential factor has fallen to \(\mathrm e^{-80}\) there. Polynomial factors still enter the integrand; this bound is chosen for the implemented low-shell domain. Radial integrals, channel lists and each spectral line’s maximum strength are cached. The percentage divides the chosen channel’s squared spatial matrix element by the strongest allowed channel on the same spectral line, independent of active UI filters.

9. Spend computation where it changes the picture

State-dependent sample buffers are reused; bounded caches hold three orbital distributions or four transition distributions. Static cloud images can be stored in an offscreen framebuffer and copied until their density, camera, cutaway, resolution or other relevant inputs change. Phase animation invalidates that image over time; a running transition also invalidates it as population changes. Unchanged orbital frames skip drawing, and offscreen or hidden transition views stop scheduling work.

The tracer slider is a ceiling. The limiter monitors recent frame intervals and, where supported, asynchronous GPU timer queries. Sustained slow frames reduce the active count quickly; sustained headroom restores it gradually. Granular aims around 30 FPS without lowering its raster-quality tier. Cloud targets about 30 FPS in Orbits and 60 FPS in Spectra, and can also reduce its framebuffer/ray-step tier if tracer reduction is insufficient. These are feedback targets, not hardware-independent guarantees. Browser scheduling, display refresh, thermal throttling and other workloads still matter. A static frame is not treated as proof that a more expensive animated frame would meet the target.

Spin, fine structure and external fields

The Coulomb model explains the main pattern. Electron spin, relativity, nuclear magnetism and applied fields resolve that pattern into finer lines. These effects require additional state labels and are not simulated by the atlas.

\[\hat H=\hat H_{\mathrm C}+\hat H_{\mathrm{fs}}+\hat H_{\mathrm{hfs}}+\hat H_Z+\hat H_S+\cdots\]
\[\mathbf J=\mathbf L+\mathbf S,\qquad \mathbf F=\mathbf J+\mathbf I\]
Derivation & detailsFrom a spatial wavefunction to spin and field-dependent spectra

Relativity introduces a spinor

The Schrödinger wavefunction used here has one complex spatial amplitude. An electron also has intrinsic angular momentum \(s=\tfrac12\), with two spin components. Spin is not a little charged sphere rotating. Dirac's 1928 theory combines quantum dynamics with special relativity using a four-component spinor:

\[\mathrm i\hbar\partial_t\Psi_D=\left[c\boldsymbol\alpha_D\!\cdot\!\hat{\mathbf p}+\beta_Dm_ec^2+V\right]\Psi_D.\]

The matrices \(\boldsymbol\alpha_D,\beta_D\) enforce the relativistic energy–momentum relation; they are distinct from the dimensionless fine-structure constant \(\alpha=e^2/(4\pi\varepsilon_0\hbar c)\). Its low-speed, positive-energy expansion recovers the Schrödinger Hamiltonian and supplies corrections.

Three contributions to fine structure

\[\hat H_{\mathrm{fs}}=-\frac{\hat p^4}{8m_e^3c^2}+\frac{1}{2m_e^2c^2r}\frac{\mathrm dV}{\mathrm dr}\,\mathbf L\!\cdot\!\mathbf S+\frac{\hbar^2}{8m_e^2c^2}\nabla^2V.\]

The first term follows by expanding \(\sqrt{m_e^2c^4+p^2c^2}-m_ec^2\). The second couples orbital and spin angular momentum; the final Darwin term is a contact correction for a point Coulomb source. Using \(2\mathbf L\cdot\mathbf S=\mathbf J^2-\mathbf L^2-\mathbf S^2\) and taking hydrogen expectation values gives, for an infinitely massive point proton,

\[\Delta E^{\mathrm{fs}}_{nj}=\frac{m_ec^2\alpha^4}{2n^3}\left[\frac{3}{4n}-\frac{1}{j+\tfrac12}\right],\qquad j=\ell\pm\tfrac12.\]

Only \(j=\tfrac12\) exists for \(\ell=0\). This leading correction lifts some of the Coulomb degeneracy. Finite proton mass adds recoil terms; simply replacing every electron mass with a reduced mass is not a complete relativistic calculation.

Hyperfine structure and the 21-centimetre line

The proton also has spin \(I=\tfrac12\) and a magnetic moment. In a specified electronic level, its leading magnetic coupling can be written \(\hat H_{\mathrm{hfs}}=A\mathbf I\cdot\mathbf J/\hbar^2\), with \(A\) an energy. Angular-momentum addition immediately gives

\[\Delta E_F=\frac A2\left[F(F+1)-I(I+1)-J(J+1)\right].\]

For the ground state, \(I=J=\tfrac12\) allows \(F=0,1\), separated by \(A\). Their magnetic-dipole transition produces hydrogen's approximately 21 cm radio line. It does not change \(n\) and is absent from the atlas's electric-dipole line list.

Zeeman and Stark effects

A magnetic field interacts with magnetic moments. Ignoring nuclear magnetism initially, a uniform field along \(z\) adds

\[\hat H_Z=\frac{\mu_BB}{\hbar}(L_z+g_sS_z),\qquad \mu_B=\frac{e\hbar}{2m_e}.\]

When fine structure is resolved and hyperfine coupling can be neglected, weak-field shifts are \(\Delta E=\mu_Bg_Jm_JB\), where \(g_J\) projects the orbital and spin moments onto \(\mathbf J\). If hyperfine structure matters, use \(F,m_F\) and its corresponding magnetic factor instead. Stronger fields mix or decouple these angular momenta, so one diagonalizes the combined Hamiltonian.

A uniform electric field \(\mathcal E\) adds \(\hat H_S=e\mathcal E z\) for an electron. A nondegenerate parity eigenstate has \(\langle z\rangle=0\), so its first-order shift vanishes. Second-order perturbation theory gives

\[\Delta E_a^{(2)}=e^2\mathcal E^2\sum_{b\ne a}\frac{|\langle b|z|a\rangle|^2}{E_a-E_b}.\]

The complete sum includes continuum states. Degenerate excited Coulomb levels instead require diagonalizing \(z\) within their shared-energy subspace, allowing a linear Stark effect. A chosen orbital's \(m\) label alone therefore does not specify a full magnetic or electric response.

QED, the proton and precision tests

At high precision, hydrogen measures the quantum electromagnetic field and the structure of its nucleus. Known omitted effects and unresolved experimental discrepancies are different things.

\[E=E_{\mathrm C}+\Delta E_{\mathrm{rel}}+\Delta E_{\mathrm{recoil}}+\Delta E_{\mathrm{QED}}+\Delta E_{\mathrm{nuclear}}+\cdots\]
\[\Delta E_{\mathrm{size}}(nS)\simeq\frac{2\pi}{3}\frac{e^2}{4\pi\varepsilon_0}\,r_p^2\,|\psi_{n00}(0)|^2\]
Derivation & detailsLamb shift, isotope shifts and what remains uncertain

Two moving particles, not one fixed source

Separating centre-of-mass and relative motion replaces \(m_e\) by \(\mu=m_em_p/(m_e+m_p)\) in the nonrelativistic relative-coordinate problem. Thus \(a_\mu=4\pi\varepsilon_0\hbar^2/(\mu e^2)\) and \(E_n=-\mu c^2\alpha^2/(2n^2)\). The atlas's fixed-proton energy convention uses \(m_e\), making its displayed wavelengths illustrative Coulomb values, not precision laboratory line positions.

Replacing the proton with a deuteron changes the reduced mass and nuclear properties. The leading isotope shift follows directly from the mass change; finite nuclear size, magnetic structure and recoil corrections then refine it. Quantum numbers can label similar spatial patterns while measured frequencies differ.

Why Dirac's equation is not the last step

Lamb and Retherford's 1947 experiment resolved a separation between the \(2S_{1/2}\) and \(2P_{1/2}\) levels that are degenerate in the point-nucleus Dirac spectrum. Coupling the atom to a quantized electromagnetic field produces radiative corrections, including electron self-energy and vacuum polarization, that help explain this Lamb shift.

Quantum electrodynamics, QED, is a quantum field theory: electrons, positrons and photons are field excitations. “QFT” is the framework, not an additional correction competing with QED. Calculations organize observables in powers of coupling and account for renormalized masses and charges. A classical oscillating driving field can model stimulated transitions, but spontaneous emission and photon correlations require the quantum field or a justified open-system approximation.

The nucleus has a finite charge distribution

Let \(r_p^2\) denote the mean-square proton charge radius. Expanding its electrostatic potential about a point charge gives, at leading order, a contact perturbation

\[\delta V(\mathbf r)\simeq\frac{2\pi}{3}\frac{e^2}{4\pi\varepsilon_0}r_p^2\delta^{(3)}(\mathbf r),\qquad |\psi_{n00}(0)|^2=\frac{1}{\pi n^3a_\mu^3}.\]

Taking its expectation value produces the featured size correction. The positive shift means the extended nucleus binds an S electron slightly less strongly than a point charge. Nonrelativistic states with \(\ell>0\) vanish at the origin, so their leading contact contribution vanishes. Precision theory also includes higher moments, nuclear polarizability and two-photon exchange.

Line positions are only part of a spectrum

An unstable excited state has a finite lifetime. If its population decays as \(\exp(-t/\tau)\), its probability amplitude decays as \(\exp[-t/(2\tau)]\). Fourier transforming that amplitude gives a Lorentzian natural line with frequency full width \(\Delta\nu=1/(2\pi\tau)\), for decay to a sufficiently sharp lower level. Doppler motion, collisions, driving intensity and instrument response add other broadening mechanisms. The narrow drawn spectral marks identify idealized transitions; they are not predictions of these experimental line profiles.

What is still being tested?

Different transitions weight the Rydberg constant, proton radius and QED terms differently. Fitting several measured frequencies therefore tests their mutual consistency; fitting one unknown to one observation is not an independent test. The proton-radius puzzle arose when muonic-hydrogen spectroscopy disagreed with earlier radius determinations.

A 2026 atomic-hydrogen measurement of the 2S–6P transition, combined with 1S–2S, obtained \(r_p=0.8406(15)\,\mathrm{fm}\), agreeing with the muonic value. This substantially strengthens the smaller-radius result without making every older measurement agree. Remaining work concerns experimental systematics, higher-order bound-state QED, nuclear structure and independent cross-checks; it is not evidence that the basic orbital solution has failed.

Where the discrete spectrum ends

For \(E<0\), demanding a regular, decaying Coulomb wavefunction selects discrete energies. At \(E>0\), solutions oscillate at large radius instead. They describe scattering or escaping electrons and use continuum normalization, or normalizable wave packets assembled from continuum states. The energy threshold is not a final giant orbital.

Absorbing a sufficiently energetic photon can ionize hydrogen. Energy conservation supplies the second featured equation, including recoil of the remaining ion. The cross section additionally depends on continuum matrix elements and polarization. Multiphoton ionization, tunnelling in a strong field and recombination require dynamics beyond the atlas's bound-pair animation.

Rydberg states magnify the atom

Writing \(C=\mu c^2\alpha^2/2\), the large-\(n\) spacing follows by subtracting consecutive \(-C/n^2\) energies and expanding in \(1/n\): \(\Delta E\simeq2C/n^3\). Size grows while binding and level spacing shrink, making Rydberg states sensitive to stray fields. Lifetimes depend on the angular momentum and accessible decay paths, not only the shell number.

A useful scale for field ionization comes from the Coulomb-plus-static-field potential along the escape direction. In atomic units, \(V(r)=-1/r-Fr\) has a saddle at \(r=F^{-1/2}\), with \(V_{\mathrm{saddle}}=-2\sqrt F\). Equating this with \(-1/(2n^2)\) gives \(F\sim1/(16n^4)\). This is an over-barrier estimate, not a sharp universal threshold: Stark shifts, tunnelling, pulse shape and state preparation matter.

Change the constituents, change the scales

Muonic hydrogen replaces the electron with a negatively charged muon. Its much larger reduced mass contracts the atom by roughly two orders of magnitude and greatly amplifies sensitivity to the proton's size. The finite-size term scales as \(|\psi(0)|^2\propto\mu^3\); muon decay and additional precision corrections also matter.

Antihydrogen consists of an antiproton and a positron. Comparing matched transitions with hydrogen tests matter–antimatter symmetry, including CPT, after accounting for the magnetic trapping fields. ALPHA has measured its 1S–2S hyperfine components; these are precision experiments, not different orbital shapes predicted by the ordinary Coulomb model.

Positronium binds an electron to a positron, giving \(\mu=m_e/2\) in the leading Coulomb problem, but annihilation introduces decay channels absent in hydrogen. The negative hydrogen ion, \(\mathrm H^-\), instead adds a second electron; electron correlation prevents reducing it to a single hydrogen orbital with a changed mass.

One more proton changes the symmetry

For \(\mathrm H_2^+\), one electron feels two attractive centres plus proton–proton repulsion. Even this simple molecule no longer has the spherical symmetry of one proton. Adding a second electron gives \(\mathrm H_2\), introducing electron repulsion and the antisymmetry required for identical fermions. Molecular orbitals, correlations and nuclear motion determine bonding, vibration, rotation and dissociation.

The second equation is the leading Born–Oppenheimer separation: for fixed nuclear coordinates \(\mathbf R\), solve the electronic problem, then use its energy as a potential for the slower nuclei. Neglecting couplings between electronic surfaces is an approximation justified by a suitable separation of scales. Near crossings or during rapid motion, nonadiabatic couplings can become essential.

Dense matter and plasma

Heating and compression change dissociation, ionization and the balance of molecular and atomic populations. A plasma contains interacting charged particles whose screening, collisions and collective fields affect spectra. In dense hydrogen, neighboring electronic states overlap and independent isolated-atom orbitals cease to be a sufficient description. Numerical methods must treat many-body correlations and nuclear motion, with convergence and approximation errors assessed separately.

What “metallic hydrogen” means

Metallicity concerns electronic transport in a bulk phase. It is not a special excited orbital or simply a collection of atoms with large \(n\). A conducting fluid and a cold crystalline metal are different thermodynamic states; molecular dissociation and the onset of metallic conduction need not be the same boundary in every regime.

Shock-compression experiments reported metallic conductivity in hot dense fluid hydrogen in 1996. Low-temperature, high-pressure solid hydrogen poses different experimental challenges. A 2025 diffraction study reported structural evolution toward molecular association at ultrahigh pressure, adding evidence about the route toward atomic metallic hydrogen rather than establishing every proposed metallic phase.

Claims about cold solid metallic hydrogen require scrutiny of pressure calibration, sample identity, structure and electrical or optical evidence. Predicted superconductivity and possible survival after pressure release are separate questions requiring their own evidence. A prediction of either is not a demonstrated property of a recoverable material.

Which questions require more than atomic theory?

The many-particle Hamiltonian above is still nonrelativistic and treats nuclei as particles with specified masses and charges. It does not derive the proton from quarks and gluons. That deeper nuclear structure belongs to quantum chromodynamics and enters precision atomic work through measured or calculated nuclear properties. Likewise, predicting a dense-material phase diagram requires free energies and statistical mechanics across temperature and pressure, not merely a solution for one stationary electronic state.

Keep the model's boundary explicit

The mathematical sequence is therefore conditional: specify particles and interactions, choose an approximation, solve with appropriate boundary conditions, calculate observables, and compare with experiment.
